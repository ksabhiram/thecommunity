Hydrothermal Synthesis Calculation

Enter the target formula and amount, then the precursors from your procedure. Oxidation states are worked out automatically from the formulas, so you don't need them in the procedure.

1. Target product

Decimals work in the target: Bi0.25Sb3, Bi0.45Sb1.55Te3. If both mass and mmol are filled, mmol is used.

2. Starting precursors

One precursor per target element. Rows are pre-filled with common choices, so replace them with what your procedure uses. Hydrates are fine: CuCl2·2H2O or CuCl2.2H2O.

ElementPrecursor formulaInitial OSTarget OSRole
Oxidation states (OS) are auto-derived from charge balance. Initial OS comes from the precursor, and target OS from the product formula. Anything marked “enter manually” could not be inferred safely. You can overwrite any value, and clearing a field returns it to auto.

3. Reducing agent

Set its electron capacity in the field above.
Excess factor still multiplies this.
Important: electron capacity is an ideal bookkeeping model. Real hydrothermal systems lose reductant to hydrolysis, decomposition and side reactions, and capacity depends on the end product (e.g. phosphite vs phosphate). Treat the result as an estimate, not a validated recipe.

Built-in checks

What this calculator guards against
  • Invalid, empty, or unknown-element formulas; non-positive quantities.
  • Decimal stoichiometry and hydrates are parsed separately, so Bi0.45 is never mistaken for a hydrate.
  • Every target element needs a precursor that contains it, and no other target element.
  • Oxidation states are inferred from charge balance (nitrate, sulfate, acetate, oxo-anions, halides, hydrates). Ambiguous cases are flagged.
  • If any oxidation state is missing, the NaBH₄ result is withheld instead of silently under-counting electrons.
  • Oxidation steps (initial OS lower than target) are flagged and excluded from the NaBH₄ count.
For planning only. Verify precursor purity, hydrate state, oxidation states, and the published method before lab work. Reducing agents (NaBH₄ releases hydrogen and reacts vigorously with water and acids; hydrazine is toxic) require appropriate institutional safety controls.
